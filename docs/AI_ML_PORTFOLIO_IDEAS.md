# Should Andre Weissmann Add ML/AI to His Data Portfolio?

**Prepared for:** Andre Weissmann — healthcare analytics job search, Chicago
**Portfolio:** andre-weissmann-data-portfolio-live.pplx.app
**Context:** Existing SQL sandbox (sql.js), BMI calculator, deep-dive case studies (e.g., Nashville Housing data-cleaning project), Power BI/Tableau/Excel work. Building DataGlow (local-first analytics product) separately. Skills: SQL, Python, healthcare domain (HIPAA, claims, ICD/CPT). Not claiming an ML engineer title.

---

## 1. Direct Answer: **Selective yes**

Add AI/ML — but narrowly, and only where it reinforces the story "healthcare analytics professional who understands data trust, governance, and applied statistics," not "person who wrapped an OpenAI API call in a chat box."

The 2024–2026 hiring data is unambiguous on two fronts simultaneously:

- **Domain-relevant AI fluency is now a differentiator, not a nice-to-have.** Guidance aimed at data/analytics candidates in 2025–2026 explicitly lists "at least one project that uses LLMs, RAG, or Hugging Face transformers in a practical context" as one of three things separating portfolios that get callbacks from ones that get skipped, alongside domain relevance and live deployment ([Tutort/LinkedIn, "Your Data Science Portfolio Is Outdated"](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/your-data-science-portfolio-outdated-do-get-hired-2026-tutort-k8nsc)). Hiring-manager-sourced guides consistently rank "GenAI awareness" and "deployment and AI awareness" among emerging 2025–2026 expectations for data candidates ([Medium/Data Science Collective portfolio guide](https://medium.com/data-science-collective/ultimate-guide-to-building-a-data-science-portfolio-that-lands-interviews-1770c7a31091)).
- **At the same time, there is an active, well-documented backlash against AI-flavored job-search artifacts that feel generic, unverifiable, or hollow.** A TopResume survey of 600 U.S. hiring managers found 33.5% can spot AI-generated material within ~20 seconds, and about 1 in 5 would reject a candidate outright for it ([U.S. Chamber of Commerce coverage of TopResume survey](https://www.uschamber.com/co/run/human-resources/hiring-ai-job-applications); [Clever.cv, "AI-Slop Resume Backlash"](https://www.clever.cv/blog/ai-slop-resume-backlash-recruiters-reject-ai-resumes-2026)). Robert Half's 2026 research found 65% of hiring managers say AI-generated materials make hiring *harder*, and 82% of hiring managers report distrust of candidates who lean on AI in their job search even though their own companies use AI to screen ([Robert Half/PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/robert-half-nearly-two-thirds-of-managers-say-ai-generated-resumes-are-making-hiring-harder-302725546.html); [Forbes, "The AI Hiring Paradox"](https://www.forbes.com/sites/williamarruda/2026/07/16/the-ai-hiring-paradox-employers-use-ai-but-dont-trust-candidates-who-do/)).

**The resolution:** the backlash is against *content generated by AI to fake competence* (resumes, cover letters, boilerplate chatbots). It is not against *demonstrated, working ML/AI engineering* with visible tradeoffs, evaluation, and caveats. A recruiter can tell the difference between "I pasted a prompt into GPT-4 and called it a feature" and "I built a browser-based risk classifier, documented its precision/recall, and explained why I wouldn't trust it in production." The first triggers the AI-slop reflex; the second is exactly the "practical GenAI/ML fluency" signal that 2025–2026 guides say is now expected.

Given Andre's profile — strong SQL, healthcare domain knowledge (HIPAA, claims, ICD/CPT), Python, no ML engineer title claimed, and a separate product (DataGlow) already carrying the "AI-adjacent, local-first" torch — the right posture is: **add 1–2 small, well-scoped, transparently-caveated ML/AI features that lean on his existing domain strength**, not a suite of flashy generative features that compete with DataGlow or read as a wrapper.

## 2. Tier List

### Do Now
1. **Risk-score / classification demo on public healthcare data** (readmission risk, no-show prediction, or claims-denial risk) — logistic regression / gradient boosting, shown with a model card (metrics, limitations, fairness caveat).
2. **Data-quality AI assist bolted onto the existing SQL sandbox** — anomaly/outlier flagging or schema-aware "explain this weird value" feature layered on the Nashville Housing or a healthcare claims dataset he already knows.
3. **Prompt-to-SQL with verification**, scoped to the existing sql.js sandbox — natural language question → generated SQL → **executed against the real data and shown next to the result**, so correctness is self-evident rather than asserted.

### Do Later (only if the above land well and he wants to go further)
4. **Browser-based NLP on synthetic clinical notes** (Transformers.js sentiment/entity extraction on de-identified/synthetic text, e.g., Synthea-generated notes) — cool and on-brand, but needs more polish and clearer caveats to avoid looking like a toy.
5. **An "evals" page/writeup** showing how he tested a prompt-to-SQL or classification feature (accuracy on held-out queries, failure cases) — very credible with technical hiring managers, but only worth building once he has at least one AI feature to evaluate.

### Skip
6. Generic embedded chatbot ("Ask me about my resume!") with no grounding or verification.
7. Any AI feature that ingests or implies real/identifiable patient data.
8. Full generative "diagnosis" or "treatment recommendation" demos — a credibility and liability minefield with zero upside for an analytics (not clinical) role.
9. Anything that duplicates DataGlow's core product surface (e.g., a full local-first AI analytics assistant) unless done deliberately as a case study *about* DataGlow, clearly labeled as a separate product.

## 3. Concrete Feature Ideas (Effort / Credibility / Risk / Healthcare Fit)

### Idea 1 — 30-Day Readmission Risk Classifier
- **What:** Logistic regression or gradient-boosted tree on the public Diabetes 130-US hospitals dataset (or similar de-identified claims-style data) predicting 30-day readmission risk, with an interactive "patient profile" slider that updates a risk score live.
- **Effort:** Medium (2–4 days). Public dataset (Diabetes 130-Hospitals, UCI/Kaggle) is already used in comparable portfolio projects, e.g., a readmission-prediction case study using ~101,766 records and multiple classifiers with SMOTE for class imbalance and ROC/F1 evaluation ([example portfolio project](https://sakib1605.github.io/portfolio/Project_7)).
- **Credibility signal:** High — this is a real, well-known healthcare analytics problem (reducing readmission penalties under CMS rules). Shows he understands classification metrics (precision/recall/F1, ROC-AUC, confusion matrix), not just that he called `model.fit()`.
- **Risk/gimmick score:** Low, *if* he includes a visible model card: features used, accuracy/AUC, known limitations (dataset age, demographic bias, "not for clinical use" disclaimer). Without that, it's a Kaggle-tutorial retread — hiring guides specifically warn that "Titanic-style" or generic Kaggle projects no longer impress ([365DataScience](https://365datascience.com/career-advice/how-to-build-a-data-science-portfolio/), [DataScienceHired](https://datasciencehired.com/blog/data-science-portfolio-projects-that-get-you-hired/)).
- **Healthcare fit:** Excellent — directly maps to his claims/ICD/CPT background and a problem hospitals actually pay analysts to solve.

### Idea 2 — Prompt-to-SQL with Live Execution Verification (built into existing sandbox)
- **What:** A text box beside the existing SQL sandbox: user types "show me the top 5 most expensive claims by CPT code," an LLM (or a constrained rules/embedding-based mapper for a fully offline version) generates SQL, and it runs immediately against the same sql.js database, showing the generated query *and* its result side by side with the "ground truth" manual query.
- **Effort:** Medium-high (3–5 days) for a robust version; low (1–2 days) for a scoped version limited to a handful of schema-aware templates.
- **Credibility signal:** Very high. This is close to what production text-to-SQL systems do — schema grounding plus execution-based verification is exactly the pattern Microsoft's engineering team describes as critical to accuracy (removing runtime execution checking dropped accuracy from 80% to 38% in their benchmarks) ([Microsoft ISE Dev Blog](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/ise/llm-sql-query-generation/)) and is the core idea behind academic text-to-SQL verification research (SQL-PaLM's "verify with execution" step) ([SQL-PaLM paper, OpenReview](https://openreview.net/notes/edits/attachment?id=WyhWX2LvGE&name=pdf)). Showing the *verification step explicitly* (query ran, returned N rows, matches expectation) is what separates this from a chatbot toy.
- **Risk/gimmick score:** Low if verification is visible; **high if it's just "type a question, get an answer" with no way to check correctness** — that reads as an unverified LLM wrapper, which is precisely the "ChatGPT wrapper" pattern that draws skepticism.
- **Healthcare fit:** Strong — directly extends his existing, already-praised SQL sandbox rather than adding an unrelated feature. Also maps to a real analyst pain point (translating stakeholder questions into queries).

### Idea 3 — Data Quality / Anomaly-Detection Assist on the Sandbox Dataset
- **What:** A lightweight anomaly flag pass over the Nashville Housing (or a healthcare claims) dataset — flag rows where sale price is a statistical outlier for the parcel's land-use category, or where an ICD/CPT code combination is implausible, using z-score/IQR or an Isolation Forest, then let the user click through flagged rows.
- **Effort:** Low-medium (1–3 days). This is a natural extension of the data-cleaning narrative he already tells (56,477 corrupt records → trusted asset).
- **Credibility signal:** High for analytics roles specifically — data quality and anomaly detection are increasingly framed as core AI-assisted analyst work, not just "ML engineer" territory. Industry data-quality vendors treat AI-driven anomaly detection as a mainstream feature for the data-analyst-adjacent workflow, not an exotic one ([DQLabs](https://www.dqlabs.ai/ml-based-anomaly-detection/); [Planisware on AI-driven data quality](https://planisware.com/resources/artificial-intelligence-ppm/5-ai-features-transforming-strategic-project-and-portfolio)).
- **Risk/gimmick score:** Low — it's simple statistics dressed as "AI," but framed honestly ("simple statistical anomaly detection, not a black box") it reads as mature judgment rather than oversell.
- **Healthcare fit:** Strong conceptually (claims fraud/anomaly detection is a real healthcare analytics function) even if built first on the housing dataset; a healthcare-claims version would be even stronger.

### Idea 4 — Browser-Based De-identified Clinical Note Classifier (Transformers.js)
- **What:** A small, on-device NLP demo — e.g., sentiment/urgency classification or named-entity highlighting (medications, conditions) on **synthetic** clinical notes (Synthea-generated, never real PHI), running fully client-side via Transformers.js so no text ever leaves the browser.
- **Effort:** Medium (2–4 days) using a pre-trained model (e.g., a small DistilBERT-based classifier or existing biomedical NER model converted to ONNX) via Hugging Face's Transformers.js, which runs models "entirely in the browser—no backend model server required" ([OpenReplay guide to Transformers.js](https://blog.openreplay.com/run-ai-models-browser-transformers-js/); [Hugging Face Transformers.js v4 announcement](https://huggingface.co/blog/samihalawa/transformers-js)).
- **Credibility signal:** Medium-high, mainly because it's technically interesting (on-device inference, WebGPU/WASM, no API key) and directly answers the "on-device vs. API" question in a privacy-forward way. TensorFlow.js's own team notes fitness/health startups specifically favor client-side ML for privacy reasons ([TensorFlow.js pros/cons discussion, Google AI Forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/discuss-pros-cons-between-tensorflow-core-and-tensorflow-js-please/19016)).
- **Risk/gimmick score:** Medium. NLP-on-notes demos are common enough to feel unoriginal if under-explained, and healthcare NLP has real failure modes (negation handling, ambiguous abbreviations) that must be called out or it looks naive. Must use clearly synthetic data with an explicit label ("synthetic notes, generated by Synthea — no real patient data was used") — synthetic clinical text for NLP work is an established, published practice ([Synthea-based synthetic clinical NLP corpus, D-Nb](https://d-nb.info/1243411538/34); [arXiv, "Towards a Large Corpus of Synthetically-Identified Clinical Notes"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.02728v1.pdf)).
- **Healthcare fit:** Strong subject matter, but this is the feature most likely to be misread as "claims ML engineering skill" if not carefully scoped — keep it labeled as an exploratory/technical demo, not a "product."

### Idea 5 — LLM Eval Writeup for the Prompt-to-SQL Feature
- **What:** A short, technical "How I tested this" page: a held-out set of 15–20 natural-language questions against the sandbox schema, pass/fail on exact-match or execution-match, plus 2–3 documented failure cases and why they failed.
- **Effort:** Low (1 day) once Idea 2 exists.
- **Credibility signal:** Very high with technical reviewers. Evals are the single most-repeated best practice in 2025 LLM engineering guidance — the core idea is that you don't just ship an LLM feature, you show a "defined scope, decontaminated, diverse" test set and report metrics ([arXiv, "A Practical Guide for Evaluating LLMs and LLM-Reliant Systems"](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.13023v2); [Datadog LLM evaluation framework guide](https://www.datadoghq.com/blog/llm-evaluation-framework-best-practices/)). Almost no entry-level or career-switcher portfolios do this, so it's a strong differentiator with minimal build cost.
- **Risk/gimmick score:** Very low — this is the opposite of gimmicky; it's the thing that makes an AI feature look engineered rather than demoed.
- **Healthcare fit:** Indirect but valuable — shows the rigor mindset healthcare analytics (with its compliance/audit culture) rewards.

### Idea 6 — "Model Card" / Limitations Panel Attached to Any ML Feature
- **What:** Not a standalone feature, but a mandatory UI pattern: every ML/AI feature gets a small, visible panel stating what data trained/powers it, known limitations, and an explicit "not for clinical decision-making" disclaimer.
- **Effort:** Very low (a few hours per feature).
- **Credibility signal:** High — signals HIPAA/compliance awareness, which is one of Andre's actual stated strengths and something generic bootcamp portfolios never include.
- **Risk/gimmick score:** Essentially zero risk; this is pure upside and should be treated as non-optional for any AI/ML addition given the healthcare framing.
- **Healthcare fit:** Perfect — this is the exact kind of governance-mindedness that distinguishes a healthcare-domain analyst from a generalist and pairs naturally with his HIPAA/claims background.

### Idea 7 (situational) — No-Show / Appointment Prediction Model
- **What:** Classification model predicting patient no-show risk using a public no-show dataset (e.g., the well-known Brazilian medical appointment no-show dataset), with a simple "what factors drive this" feature-importance chart (SHAP or coefficients).
- **Effort:** Low-medium (1–3 days) — well-trodden dataset, straightforward classification task.
- **Credibility signal:** Medium-high — a real, non-clinical, operationally meaningful healthcare analytics problem (scheduling optimization, revenue impact from no-shows) that doesn't touch sensitive clinical content at all, lowering privacy risk while staying on-domain.
- **Risk/gimmick score:** Low, though it's a fairly common dataset — differentiate via the operational framing (e.g., $ impact, staffing implications) rather than the ML technique itself, per the general "business relevance beats fancier ML" pattern hiring guides emphasize ([DataScienceHired guide](https://datasciencehired.com/blog/data-science-portfolio-projects-that-get-you-hired/)).
- **Healthcare fit:** Strong and lower-risk than clinical NLP because it avoids notes/PHI-adjacent content entirely.

### Idea 8 (situational) — Claims Denial Risk Score
- **What:** If Andre can construct or find a synthetic/public claims dataset, a model scoring likelihood of claim denial based on CPT/ICD code combinations, payer, and documentation completeness fields.
- **Effort:** Medium-high (data availability is the bottleneck — may require synthetic data generation).
- **Credibility signal:** Very high specifically for healthcare-analytics recruiters, since claims denial prediction is a widely-recognized, budget-relevant use case in revenue cycle management.
- **Risk/gimmick score:** Low technically, but execution risk is higher because public labeled claims-denial data is scarce; a poorly-labeled synthetic version could look hand-wavy. Only pursue if he can be transparent about how labels were generated.
- **Healthcare fit:** Best domain fit of any idea on this list, directly leveraging his stated ICD/CPT/claims expertise — worth the extra effort if feasible.

## 4. What NOT to Add

- **A generic embedded chatbot ("Ask me anything about my resume").** This is the single most-cited example of a portfolio feature that reads as a thin LLM wrapper rather than a demonstrated skill — it doesn't showcase SQL, statistics, or domain judgment, and it's exactly the kind of AI surface that triggers the current hiring-manager skepticism toward "AI slop" ([Clever.cv, AI-slop backlash](https://www.clever.cv/blog/ai-slop-resume-backlash-recruiters-reject-ai-resumes-2026)).
- **Any feature using real or even loosely de-identified patient data.** Given his stated HIPAA awareness, using anything other than clearly synthetic (Synthea-style) or well-established public/anonymized research datasets (e.g., UCI Diabetes 130-Hospitals) would be a serious credibility and ethics misstep. Synthetic-data practice for clinical NLP is well established precisely to avoid this ([D-Nb synthetic clinical corpus paper](https://d-nb.info/1243411538/34)).
- **Clinical decision-support framing** — anything implying "this tells a doctor what to do" (diagnosis suggestions, treatment recommendations, triage prioritization presented as authoritative). This crosses from analytics into medical-device-adjacent territory and invites liability and credibility questions with no upside for an analytics role.
- **Full generative "AI insights" dashboards** that auto-write narrative summaries of arbitrary data with no visible verification — these are the most common current example of superficial AI-as-decoration and are widely flagged as vague/unverifiable in 2025–2026 hiring commentary.
- **Anything that duplicates DataGlow's product surface.** If DataGlow's pitch is local-first analytics with embedded AI, a portfolio feature offering the same value proposition dilutes both narratives and can look like Andre is job-hunting with his own product's homework. If a DataGlow-adjacent capability is shown at all, it should be an explicit case study *about* DataGlow (labeled as a separate product he's building), not folded silently into the personal portfolio's own feature set.
- **Claiming ML engineer skills he doesn't have.** Titles and framing matter: per hiring-manager guidance, specificity beats inflated titles ("Data Analyst with applied ML for healthcare risk scoring" vs. implying "Machine Learning Engineer") ([LinkFolio 2025 portfolio guide](https://linkfolio.cv/blog/data-scientist-portfolio-guide-2025)). Every AI feature should be framed as "applied ML for an analytics use case," never as evidence of ML engineering seniority.

## 5. How AI/ML Should Sit Next to the Existing Deep Dives

- **Position, don't replace.** The SQL sandbox, BMI calculator, Power BI/Tableau/Excel work, and Nashville Housing case study currently carry the core message: *this person can take messy real data and make it trustworthy and useful.* AI/ML features should be additive proof points under that same thesis — e.g., "I clean the data (existing case study) → I query it fluently (existing SQL sandbox) → I can also apply lightweight ML/AI on top of it, responsibly (new)." The AI section should visually and narratively sit *after* the SQL/BI work, framed as "applied extensions," not as a headline replacement.
- **Reuse, don't duplicate, datasets.** The strongest version of Idea 2 (prompt-to-SQL) and Idea 3 (anomaly detection) extend the *same* Nashville Housing sandbox and/or a healthcare claims dataset already featured, rather than introducing an unrelated toy dataset. This reinforces depth (multiple angles on data he clearly understands) instead of breadth-for-its-own-sake, which hiring guides consistently favor — "3 excellent projects" beats "10 generic ones" ([DataScienceHired's "3-Project Portfolio" framework](https://datasciencehired.com/blog/data-science-portfolio-projects-that-get-you-hired/)).
- **Keep the section modest in visual weight.** Given his audience is healthcare analytics hiring managers (not ML research teams), 1–3 well-documented AI features with model cards will land better than a large, showy "AI Lab" section. The existing tone of the site — professional, technical, data-cleaning/trust-focused — should carry through: each AI feature needs the same rigor (methods, caveats, results) currently given to the Nashville Housing cleaning writeup.
- **Explicitly separate from DataGlow.** If DataGlow is mentioned in the portfolio at all (e.g., in an "other projects" or "what I'm building" section), it should be clearly labeled as a separate, ongoing product — distinct from the demonstrable-skills features on the main portfolio. This avoids the appearance that the portfolio is a de facto demo environment for DataGlow, and keeps the portfolio's job-search purpose distinct from the product's fundraising/customer purpose.
- **Lead every AI feature with the same "trust" framing as the Nashville Housing project.** That case study's power comes from showing seven concrete, auditable data-cleaning techniques with clear before/after evidence. AI/ML features should mirror this: show the method, show the verification/evaluation, show the caveats — never just show a clean-looking output with no visible working.
