The Question

What do data professionals actually earn, use, and feel about their careers?

Job postings and salary aggregators tell you what companies say they pay. They don't tell you what the people already in the field report earning, what tools they reach for on their own, or whether they'd call the work worth it. This project uses Alex the Analyst's Data Professional Survey dataset to answer that from the inside.

The dataset matters for two audiences. For anyone hiring data talent, it is a reality check on what candidates expect and where they're likely to feel shortchanged. For anyone entering the field, it is a benchmark against inflated job-posting numbers and vague "growth opportunity" pitches. Power BI was used to model the survey responses, calculate role-level and country-level metrics with DAX, and surface patterns that a raw spreadsheet would hide.

The Process

How it was built in Power BI

1

Data import and type validation

Survey exports came in with salary stored as a text range rather than a usable number. Before any modeling could happen, every column needed a validated type, and the salary field needed a conversion path from text to numeric.

2

Salary range parsing in Power Query

Respondents answered with ranges like "$60k-$80k" instead of exact figures. Power Query split each range into its low and high bound, then calculated the midpoint (in this example, 70) as a single numeric field usable in DAX measures.

3

DAX measures for averages and filtered scores

With a clean numeric salary column in place, DAX measures handled the aggregation: average salary overall, average salary sliced by role, row counts by country, and calculated satisfaction scores filtered per segment.

Avg Salary = AVERAGE('Survey'[Salary_Midpoint]) Salary by Role = CALCULATE( [Avg Salary], ALLEXCEPT('Survey', 'Survey'[Job Title]) ) // Respondent count by country, ignoring other filters Respondents by Country = CALCULATE( COUNTROWS('Survey'), ALLEXCEPT('Survey', 'Survey'[Country]) )
4

Custom dark theme, color-coded by tool

A custom Power BI theme replaced the default palette so that Python, R, and SQL each carry a consistent, distinguishable color across every visual on the page, making the language-preference story readable at a glance.

5

Cross-filtering across all six visuals

Relationships were enabled so that clicking any role, country, or language on one visual filters every other visual on the page. Selecting "Data Analyst" instantly narrows the satisfaction gauges and country breakdown to that group.

Key Findings

Four patterns worth acting on

$39k gap between Data Scientist and Data Analyst salary

Data Scientists report an average of $94k against $55k for Data Analysts. It is the largest single-step compensation jump in the field, reflecting how distinct the role boundaries are in practice.

Python chosen by 59% as favorite language

R (18%) and SQL (13%) trail by 41 points combined. SQL and R together trail Python by 41 points combined — the field has converged around a single dominant language.

4.27/10 average salary satisfaction

Below the midpoint. Professionals feel underpaid relative to their expectations across nearly every role, despite reporting mid-career-level compensation on paper.

43% found breaking into data "Neither Easy nor Difficult"

The field is accessible but not easy. The field is accessible but demands persistence — the majority of survey respondents described entry as difficult regardless of their education level.

What the Data Reveals
What the data reveals

A field defined by Python, low pay satisfaction, and a steep entry curve

The survey captured a snapshot of 630 people actively working in data across five countries. Average age: 29.87. The dominant tool: Python, chosen by 59% as their preferred language. Salary satisfaction: 4.27 out of 10 — well below the midpoint, across nearly every role. Breaking into the field: rated difficult or very difficult by the majority of respondents.

  • The $39k gap between Data Scientist and Data Analyst average salary is the largest single-step jump in the field.
  • R (18%) and SQL (13%) trail Python by a wide margin despite both being staples of formal data education.
  • Work-life balance satisfaction scored higher than salary satisfaction across all roles — professionals value flexibility even when pay falls short.
The Dashboard

What each visual actually shows

The finished Power BI report is a single-page dashboard built around cross-filtering — clicking any one visual immediately updates all others. Here is what each element does and why it was built that way.

Card 1 + Card 2
630 total participants · Average age 29.87

Two KPI cards anchored at the top of the report. These give stakeholders an immediate sense of sample size and who the average respondent is, before they touch any filter.

Stacked Column Chart
Number of Participants by Gender

Simple but necessary: most salary analyses skip gender breakdowns entirely. This chart shows whether the survey skews male-dominant and whether that skew should affect how you read the salary averages.

Funnel Chart
Survey Participants by Country

Shows geographic distribution in ranked order. The United States dominates. Filtering to any country updates every other visual on the page — useful for HR teams looking at specific markets.

Stacked Bar Charts (×2)
Top 5 Occupations (Pre-selected + User-specified)

Two separate charts handle two different data quality realities: respondents who chose from a dropdown vs. those who typed their own title. The user-specified column required standardizing 86 job titles in Power Query before it could be charted.

Stacked Column Chart
Participants by Education Level

The central question this project was built to answer: does a degree matter? Bachelor's holders (329 participants) dominate the sample. Filtering this chart by education level updates the salary visuals, letting stakeholders see compensation differences by credential directly.

Education vs. Salary

The finding stakeholders actually asked for

The central business question was whether education level is a necessary qualifier for hiring data professionals. The data gave a nuanced answer: advanced degrees correlate with higher compensation in most roles, but the relationship is not linear and PhD-level salaries reflect a very small sample.

High school grads: $85k avg (Data Architects, US)

36 respondents. A surprising ceiling for a non-degree credential, suggesting data architecture skills are valued over formal education in some organizations.

Associate's degree: $115k avg (Data Scientists, US)

16 respondents. The highest-performing credential by average salary at the Data Scientist level — a small sample, but notable.

Bachelor's degree: $93k avg (Data Scientists, US)

329 respondents — the largest group. Most candidates and most job postings land here. Solid baseline salary, but not the highest in the dataset.

Master's degree: $104k avg (Data Scientists, US)

192 respondents. A meaningful step up from bachelor's. Graduate credentials still carry a premium in data science roles specifically.

Key takeaway

Cleaning survey data is harder than cleaning transactional data

Salary came in as a text range ("$60k-$80k"), job titles came in as free text with 86 variations, and countries came in mixed between preselected dropdowns and typed responses. Each required a different Power Query approach before any DAX measure could run accurately. That preprocessing work — invisible in the final dashboard — is what made the analysis trustworthy.
