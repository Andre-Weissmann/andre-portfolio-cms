The Question

What business problem does this solve?

A bike retailer collects demographic and behavioral data on every customer who walks through the sales funnel, but rarely turns that data into a targeting strategy. Sales and marketing spend often gets spread evenly across the whole customer base, which wastes budget on segments that were never likely to convert in the first place.

This project set out to answer a narrower, more useful question.

THE QUESTION

Of 1,026 sales records, which customer profile is most likely to buy a bike, and where should sales focus?

Answering this well means the sales team can prioritize outreach toward the customers most likely to say yes, instead of treating every lead the same. That is the difference between a marketing list and a marketing strategy.

The Data

1,026 records, 13 variables

Each row represents one customer, with demographic fields (age, gender, marital status, income), household fields (children, home ownership, cars), and commute and location data, alongside the outcome variable: whether that customer purchased a bike.

The Process

From raw rows to a sales-ready segment

The analysis was built entirely in Excel, using pivot tables to isolate the variables that mattered and a dashboard to make the findings explorable.

Data audit and standardization
Checked all 1,026 rows for missing or inconsistent values, standardized categorical labels (region names, education levels), and bucketed continuous fields like Age and Income into readable tiers.
=IF(Age<=30,"Under 30",IF(Age<=54,"31-54","55+"))
Pivot table: Age Group x Purchased Bike
Built a pivot table crossing the new age buckets against the purchase outcome to see which life stage converts best. This is where the 31-54 middle-age segment first stood out.
Pivot table: Commute Distance x Purchased Bike
Cross-tabulated commute distance bands against purchase rate. This step surfaced the clearest signal in the entire dataset: a sharp peak in conversion at 2 to 5 miles.
Pivot table: Region and Income x Purchased Bike
Layered Region and Income bracket into a two-way pivot to see where geography and earning power intersect. Pacific Region customers with moderate to high income consistently outperformed other combinations.
Dashboard with charts and slicers
Assembled a single-page dashboard with three linked charts and slicers for Region, Age Group, and Commute Distance, so the findings could be filtered and re-checked interactively rather than taken on faith.
Key Findings

What the pivot tables revealed

Three variables carried almost all of the predictive signal: age group, commute distance, and income bracket. Region added a fourth layer that sharpened the picture further.

2.4x
2-5 mile commuters buy at 2.4x the rate of sub-1-mile commuters

Commute is the single strongest predictor of purchase intent. Customers who live too close to need a bike for transport, and those who live too far to consider it practical, both convert at far lower rates.

67%
Middle-aged buyers (31-54) represent 67% of all purchases

Youth and senior segments lag significantly despite similar income levels. Life stage, not just earning power, is driving the purchase decision.

3.1x
$60k+ income customers convert at 3.1x the rate of sub-$40k customers

Price sensitivity is real, but addressable through financing messaging. Income is a strong filter, though not as sharp a signal as commute distance.

#1
Pacific Region leads all regions in conversion rate

Europe and North America trail despite comparable demographics. The regional gap held even after controlling for income and age, suggesting a genuine market difference rather than a data artifact.

The Recommendation

Where to focus, starting now

Business Recommendation

Middle-aged buyers in the Pacific Region with 2 to 5 mile commutes converted at the highest rate of any demographic combination in the dataset — well above what age, income, or region alone would predict.

Commute distance of 2 to 5 miles showed a 2.4x higher conversion rate versus sub-1-mile commuters — the strongest single predictor in the data. A bike is practical for that distance range without being impractical. That nuance disappears when the data is not segmented.

Three variables — age bracket, commute distance, and income — together explain most of the variation in who buys a bike and who does not.

The Dashboard

What each element shows

The finished Excel dashboard is a single sheet with four linked charts and three slicer tools on the left. Every slicer updates every chart simultaneously, turning a static spreadsheet into an interactive tool a non-technical stakeholder can actually explore.

Chart 1
Average Income Per Purchase

Bar chart showing average income of bike buyers vs. non-buyers by gender. Filters: Occupation, Children. This chart answers the income question at a glance and shows whether the income gap between buyers and non-buyers is consistent across genders.

Chart 2
Customer Commute Distance

Line chart across commute distance bands from 0 to 10+ miles. This is where the sharpest signal in the dataset appears: a clear purchase-rate peak at 2 to 5 miles that drops off in both directions. Filters: Occupation, Children.

Chart 3
Customer Age Brackets

Bar chart using the derived Age Brackets column (Adolescent 25-30, Middle Age 31-54, Old 55-89). The 31-54 bracket dominates purchase volume. This chart only exists because of the nested IF formula that created the bucket column during cleaning.

Chart 4
Bikes Sold by Occupation with Car Ownership

Cross-tab of occupation type against purchase outcome, with car ownership layered in. Filter: Cars. This chart surfaced that management and professional occupations purchase bikes at higher rates regardless of how many cars they own, separating income signal from transportation-need signal.

Slicers (×3)
Region · Age Group · Commute Distance

Organized on the left side of the dashboard. Each slicer filters all four charts at once. A recruiter or stakeholder can narrow to Pacific Region, Middle Age, 2-5 miles and immediately see how those segments perform across all four dimensions simultaneously.

Six Questions Answered

The business questions that drove the analysis

The project was structured around six specific questions asked across all three regions. The Pacific Region answered favorably on five of the six.

$90k
Highest avg income per bike purchase

Married females, homeowners, management occupations in the Pacific Region. The strongest income-to-purchase signal in the dataset.

5-10+ mi
Farthest commute by bike

Pacific Region customers are biking the longest distances. This is a behavioral signal, not just demographic, and suggests a deeper cultural fit with active commuting.

$70k
Highest avg income for homeowners

Pacific Region female homeowners lead this metric. Home ownership correlates with a stable demographic likely to invest in fitness and commuting equipment.

10+ mi
Middle-aged females biking farthest

In the Pacific Region. This is the highest-value demographic overlap: high income, active commuter, proven buyer. The target profile for any future campaign.

Key takeaway

The most important decision in this project was creating the Age Brackets column before building any pivot table. Excel pivot tables work best with categorical fields, not continuous numbers. Bucketing 13,013 age values into three readable tiers with a nested IF formula was a small step that made every subsequent chart cleaner and faster to interpret. That kind of preprocessing decision, invisible in the final dashboard, is what separates a functional spreadsheet from a stakeholder-ready one.

13,351 raw entries → 13,013 after deduplication → 4 pivot tables → 1 recommendation: prioritize the Pacific Region.
